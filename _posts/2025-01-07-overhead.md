## A Wall of Memory (Bandwidth)
Top500 benchmarks follow DoE/DoD lines--they run [Linpack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LINPACK).
Linpack is a lot of things, but let's pretend it's [GEMM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basic_Linear_Algebra_Subprograms#Level_3).
We have to unpack a few things about how GEMM actually gets optimized, but I think I can substitute history for math.

One of my favorite papers of all time is: [The Future Fast Fourier Transform?](https://epubs.siam.org/doi/abs/10.1137/S1064827597316266?download=true&journalCode=sjoce3).
It has a lovely discussion on [FMM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fast_multipole_method), but I keep mentioning the article for the scaling analyses.
Most of all, I see this paper as marking the end of an era.
See--over time, the rate at which _arithmetic_ has gotten faster has been higher than the rate at which _memory bandwidth_ has gotten faster.

If you're above a certain age and had a conventional CS education, you may remember analyses where the time-complexity of some operation completely discounted all arithmetic except multiplications and divisions.
Even as a math student, I distinctly recall (Kincaid and Cheney)[https://bookstore.ams.org/amstext-2] doing this in their third edition.
I, uh, only remember this because I wrote a lengthy, _personal_, unsolicited review for this textbook and presented it to my department.
The review contained a phrase like, "When Julius Caesar skimmed the first edition of this book, even _he_ knew this was wrong."
Repeating a theme in my life, I had this phrase repeated back to me, followed by the words, "Do you think you're clever?"

I do, yes.
And handsome too.

The point is, over time we have looked at different dominating effects in different situations, and those effects have largely changed as the structure of computation has changed.
There are these walls, and they each grow or wane in relevance as different scaling laws catch up to each other and dominate.
In 1999 the memory bandwidth wall caught up to FFT.
Before _approximately_ that year, the year-over-year time-performance of FFT for a fixed problem size reliably followed the arithmetic scaling curve.
After that point, the determining curve was memory bandwidth.
Similarly, the wall hit DGEMM sometime in the mid 2000s (see [Goto & VanDeGeijin](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~flame/pubs/GotoTOMS_revision.pdf), although by this time obviously the hand was long dealt).

## Back to the EFLOPS
In a contemporary setting, a great part of GEMM performance comes down to the traversal of memory.
How do you chunk it up to take advantage of the [memory hierarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memory_hierarchy)?
Keep in mind, it's exceedingly common to block-decompose these matrices, so you are almost certainly splitting access contiguity on a sufficiently large problem (your memory-getter can't get the memory in one operation if all the memory it is getting is far apart).

This is spatial locality.
All sorts of hardware benefit from performing operations on spatially proximal addresses.
Don't forget--in the architectures we mostly use, memory is _linear_, but rather than simple [row-/column-major](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Row-_and_column-major_order), you can map matrices back to 1D using the conceptual equivalent of [hilbert curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert_curve) to exchange a sliver of arithmetic for better locality.
GPU texture memory has done this since at least 2008.

Now, that's probably not the full story, but it's enough of a story for me to feel satisfied.
Small tensorial operations really do get better data locality owing to the fact that the data traversal is more amenable to contiguous access.
We're looking at a class of operations which have smaller problem sizes (`4x4x4`!), better data locality, and have been pushed into the hardware when compared to GEMM.
So, yes, Rpeak here is going to mean something substantially different than it does for Top500.

In other words, if you benchmark some kind of floating point operation in a context with superior memory locality, you'll achieve better benchmark numbers because you've minimized the adverse dominating factors which detract from the performance of raw arithmetic.
For better or worse, this is where things get kind of dicey.
Getting a really great benchmark is one important, but small, part of the whole.
The rest really boils down to context.
To what degree is _that_ benchmark valuable?

# An Ode to Value
In my mind, conversations about "value" and conversations about "benchmarks" contrast with one another favorably.
A great part of my career has been about distilling just the right microbenchmark(s) to exercise the practical dynamic range of a library or application.
The extent to which those benchmarks--as a group, not individually--can serve as a leading indicator for the quality of that product can spell the success or failure of the whole thing.

It's also easy to get it wrong.
I was at an organization that shifted to a network-backed storage platform.
As preparation, it conducted precise, large-scale studies of IO throughput under various conditions.
These machines had their disks formatted and repopulated, were turned all the way off, and restarted for _every_ cold-start trial.
Ideal configurations were chosen and we started the work of adapting the platform to the hardware.

But when rubber contacted road, application behavior was suboptimal and the culprit appeared to be the disks.
The high-throughput, super-well-tested disks.
Even worse, even though the analysis was a bit hairy, the explanation was damning.
The determining factor was less to do with disk throughput and very much to do with latency.
Well, we didn't test that.

So, what value was that operation?
More generally, what _about_ value, really?

## Mass, Acceleration, Distance
Computer programs don't spawn in a vacuum and exist as primitive entities within the cosmos.
Technical showpieces and educational nonsense aside, each exists for a reason.
To do work.
Why?
Because there is value in having done work.

Let's pretend you've deduced a source of value and created a service which does the corresponding kind of work.
All right, so now you have this box and it is periodically doing a valuable thing.
I think it's informative to pause here.
The _value_ of value is in the exchange--you convert value of one kind into something else.
When you begin to rely on those exchanges, then you rely on the value, and in so doing you reduce the creation of value to--really--a requirement or a bother more than some kind of prize in itself.

I want to emphasize this point, because I think a lot of brilliant engineers need to hear it.
You are in a situation where someone's obligations are contingent on knowing something about the way which this box delivers value and consumes resources.
You went from nothing to dependence by the mere act of (checks notes) *generating value*.
In some sense, you did an objectively good and useful thing and were immediately and necessarily punished for that singular crime.

This is a cheesy setup, to be sure, but think about some of the moving parts here.
The _thing_ that creates the value is distinct from the value it creates.
Did you create a better widget-cranker?
Cool!
But don't forget; the value is cranked widgets, not specifically the means by which they are cranked.

In practical terms, what this means in the context of the contemporary zeitgeist is simple.
When you create or improve a thing, and that thing has value, you must also provide the means by which that value can be _secured_.
Because there are many, _many_, ways in which a computational product can be endangered, and most of those ways can be disarmed or predicted with good leading analysis, you become responsible for observability.
Arguably, this process is distinct from the process of creating the thing--but really, practically speaking who else could possibly do it but you?

## You Observe what you See (but How do you Smell?  Awful!)
This process of securing value involves the engagement between multiple different entities.
Ultimately, what organizations treasure is predictability and transparency.
Yes, cost optimization can strongly influence the posture of a product within a larger landscape or portfolio.
But if the pursuit of optimization is unscheduled, unschedulable, and unknowable--then outside of small startups, how could anyone ever afford to anticipate or plan such a future state?

The point is, heroics don't scale.
Processes do.
However--getting back to the discussion--the factors influencing these processes are split between agents.
On the one hand, you have an overlying organization which is responsible for framing financial tradeoffs and defining the "feel" of the thing (e.g., acceptable latency).
On the other hand, you have the implementation--how the thing consumes resources and the mechanistic tradeoffs involved.
Both of these serve as stakeholders, as well as change agents.

Now--obviously business leaders care about cost, technical leadership cares about latency and revenue, and business decisions are made by technical leadership all the time anyway!
So what point could this dumb, fake separation of concerns possibly have?
Well, what I find is this.
Typically, the success narrative around technological cost optimization tends to be owned by technical teams.
On the other hand, the success narrative around product fit and feel tends to be owned by Product/Business/Design teams.
(What about risk?)

## Measures of Consumption
As far as I know, there isn't a universally-accepted, robust, practical definition for the word "overhead."
Sometimes we use the term in a formal context, in which the desired quantity is well-constrained.
Sometimes we use the term informally, when something slows down (or does less work per unit time).
Overhead is almost never good (on the other hand, headroom _is_).
Nevertheless, it's a word many of us use and it's a concept we have to reason about.
